The quiet return of the night train

Ines Okoro

The 22:40 to the coast left with every berth taken, which nobody at the operator had planned for. They had ordered the carriages second-hand, repainted them over a winter, and printed a timetable that assumed half would run empty until spring.

What they had not counted on was how many people would rather lose a night to travel than a day. A sleeper turns the dead hours into distance. You board after dinner, and the country is rearranged while you are not looking.

A timetable written for sleep

The schedule is slower than it needs to be, on purpose. The train waits forty minutes in a siding outside the junction so that it does not arrive before the cafés open.

The attendant in the third carriage has worked the line for eleven years, first on freight. She keeps a paper list of who wants waking, and when.
